**Concept-family map. AS OF 2026-08-06.** Scope: the conceptual space around holding a
leveraged perpetual position for seconds to hours, on Jupiter Perps (jup.ag) specifically.

This is a *map*, not an implementation plan, and **not financial advice.** Every figure is

## 0. Three facts that reshape the map before the taxonomy starts

A conventional map of short-term trading would enumerate strategy styles — scalping,
market making, latency arbitrage, statistical arbitrage — and describe each. On this venue
that map is actively misleading, because three structural facts delete or reprice most of
its entries before any of them is evaluated.

**Fact 1 — Jupiter Perps is oracle-priced. There is no order book and no AMM curve.**
The JLP pool is the sole counterparty; execution price is the oracle print at the moment a
keeper fulfils the request. A $2M order and a $2k order fill at the same price. **There is
therefore no bid-ask spread to capture and no maker rebate to earn.** Every strategy whose
edge is "quote both sides and collect the spread" — market making, and most of what is
loosely called scalping — does not have a mechanism here. It is not unprofitable; it is
undefined. The edge, if any exists, has to come from predicting the *oracle path*.

To be precise about the scope of that claim: spread capture is perfectly well defined
*elsewhere on Solana* — the central limit order books and AMMs surveyed in
`solana-dex-and-amm-landscape.md` have books to quote into. The claim is about Jupiter
Perps specifically, and it is a claim about this venue's mechanism, not about the strategy.

**Fact 2 — below an hour, the toll is essentially all fee.** Carry is the term everyone
models and it is the term that stops mattering:

| Hold | Fee | Carry | Toll | Carry share of toll |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 s | 0.120% | 0.0000% | 0.120% | 0.02% |
| 30 s | 0.120% | 0.0001% | 0.120% | 0.06% |
| 1 min | 0.120% | 0.0001% | 0.120% | 0.11% |
| 5 min | 0.120% | 0.0007% | 0.121% | 0.55% |
| 15 min | 0.120% | 0.0020% | 0.122% | 1.64% |
| 1 h | 0.120% | 0.0080% | 0.128% | 6.25% |
| 4 h | 0.120% | 0.0320% | 0.152% | 21.05% |

At a one-minute hold, carry is **0.11% of the toll**. The 12 bps round-trip fee is the
whole cost. This inverts the framing in `research/LEVERAGE-ECONOMICS.md`, which is written
for holds of hours to months where carry is the dominant term and the interesting question
is duration. Here **carry is nearly free and turnover is the entire cost problem** — though
Fact 3 immediately qualifies that: duration stops being free the moment the barrier is
close, for a reason that has nothing to do with carry.

**Fact 3 — at high leverage the liquidation barrier sits inside the noise band.** At 250×
the barrier is a 0.40% adverse move, which is roughly 4σ of a single one-minute move but
only about one σ of a *seventeen-minute* move (§6). The position does not need a thesis to
be wrong to be removed from the game. It needs seventeen minutes.

Facts 2 and 3 pull in opposite directions and that tension is the real subject of this
document: **the toll punishes you for trading often, and the barrier punishes you for
staying in.** There is no holding period that escapes both, only a band that minimises the
sum, and §7 explains why this repo cannot currently locate that band empirically.

## 1. Parent domain and siblings

**Parent domain:** high-turnover systematic trading — the discipline of extracting a small
edge many times, where the per-trade edge is comparable in size to the per-trade cost.
Everything characteristic of the family follows from that ratio being near one, rather than
from any property of speed as such.

**Where "high leverage" enters:** leverage is *not* part of the parent domain's definition
and, crucially, **it does not change the break-even move** — the arithmetic in
`research/LEVERAGE-ECONOMICS.md` §1 shows `L` cancelling out of `break_even_move`. What
leverage changes is where the absorbing barrier sits and how fast a fixed dollar cost is
amortised. It is a survival parameter, not a profitability parameter.

**Sibling families** at the same level of abstraction, distinguished by what supplies the
edge:

| Sibling | What supplies the edge | Why it is not this family |
|---|---|---|
| **Market making** | The spread, plus maker rebates | Needs a two-sided book. Undefined on an oracle-priced venue. |
| **Latency arbitrage** | Being first to a known price change | Needs a race you can win; on Solana the keeper, not you, submits the fill. |
| **Statistical arbitrage** | Mean reversion in a spread between instruments | Usually multi-instrument and lower-turnover; the cost is paid twice, once per leg. |
| **Event-driven trading** | A discrete, dated information release | Turnover is set by event frequency, not by choice. |
| **Swing / position trading** | A multi-day directional thesis | Carry dominates, and this repo has actually measured it. |
| **Grid / ladder** | Oscillation around a range | Maximises trade count by construction — see `why-high-frequency-strategies-die-at-leverage`. |

**Adjacent fields worth borrowing from:** market microstructure (adverse selection, the
distinction between informed and uninformed flow), queueing and reliability engineering
(the keeper flow is a distributed two-phase commit with no delivery guarantee), and
sequential decision theory (an absorbing barrier makes expected value the wrong objective).

**The cross-cutting concept that belongs to several neighborhoods at once:** *the toll*.
It is a microstructure quantity, a venue-configuration quantity, and a strategy-design
constraint simultaneously, which is why organising the map around it works better than
organising around strategy names.

## 2. Axis A — Horizon bands and their feasibility gates

Each band has a different binding constraint. The constraint, not the strategy label, is
what determines whether anything in the band is reachable.

### Band 1 — Sub-second

**Binding gate: you do not control execution.** Two independent mechanisms remove
sub-second control:

- **The keeper flow is two transactions.** The trader submits a `PositionRequest` PDA; one
  of two keepers running in parallel detects it, validates it, and submits the fulfilment
  transaction. The position is not live until that second transaction confirms. Jupiter
  publishes **no latency figure and no TTL field** — the docs say only "a short delay."
- **Solana slot time bounds everything below it.** A single slot is the finest granularity
  at which anything can be sequenced, and transaction landing is probabilistic rather than
  guaranteed under contention.

**Consequence:** the band is not slow, it is *non-deterministic*. A strategy whose edge
decays inside the request-to-fill window cannot express itself, because the fill price is
the oracle print at an unknown later moment. Jupiter's `priceSlippage` guard converts that
uncertainty into a **cancellation** rather than a bad fill — which protects the trader's
price but means the strategy's realised trade set is conditioned on the market not having
moved, a selection effect that a naive backtest will not reproduce.

**UNVERIFIED:** the actual distribution of request-to-fill latency. No published figure
exists and this repo has not measured it. Any sub-second claim rests on that unmeasured
distribution.

### Band 2 — Seconds to minutes

**Binding gate: the toll, multiplied by turnover.** This is where the family's central
arithmetic bites, and it is brutal:

| Interval | Round trips/day | 1x | 5x | 10x | 20x |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 min | 1,440 | 173% | 864% | 1,728% | 3,456% |
| 5 min | 288 | 35% | 173% | 346% | 691% |
| 15 min | 96 | 12% | 58% | 115% | 230% |
| 1 h | 24 | 3% | 14% | 29% | 58% |
| 4 h | 6 | 1% | 4% | 7% | 14% |

Read the top-left cell first, because it is the one that ends most of this conversation:
**a strategy continuously deployed with a one-minute holding period pays 173% of its
collateral in fees per day at 1× leverage.** Not at 10×. At 1×. Leverage multiplies an
already-fatal number.

This is the same multiplication documented in
`~/.claude/skills/trading-and-investing/references/why-high-frequency-strategies-die-at-leverage.md`
(`fee_cost% = round_trip_fee × leverage × trade_count`), evaluated at the turnover levels
that "extremely short term" actually implies rather than at the tens-of-trades levels used
there.

### Band 3 — Minutes to hours

**Binding gate: the barrier, if leverage is high; the edge, if it is not.** This is the
only band where the toll is survivable — at a 1-hour interval and 5× leverage, fees are 14%
of collateral per day, which is merely very bad rather than arithmetically impossible.

But it is also the band where §6's barrier analysis starts to bind: at 100× leverage, one σ
of ordinary noise reaches the liquidation price in **1.8 hours**. A strategy holding for an
hour at 100× is making a bet on the noise, not on its signal.

**The band is a squeeze, not a sweet spot.** Toll pressure falls as you extend the hold;
barrier pressure rises. Where the sum is minimised depends on the volatility of the asset
and the size of the edge — neither of which this repo has measured at this frequency.

## 3. Axis B — The toll, decomposed

Three cost terms, with different scaling behaviour. Getting the scaling right matters more
than getting any single magnitude right.

```
toll_per_round_trip = fee_rt + rate·util·hours + 2·fixed_tx_cost/notional
                      ^^^^^^   ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^   ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^
                      flat in  linear in hold    inverse in notional
                      notional  (negligible <1h)  (the one term leverage helps)
```

### The fee term — flat, and therefore the binding one

12 bps round trip (0.06% each way), charged on **notional**, not collateral. Because it is
proportional to notional and leverage multiplies notional for a fixed collateral, the fee
cost *as a share of collateral* is `12 bps × L × trade_count`. Both multipliers are chosen
by the strategy designer, which is the good news and the bad news.

### The carry term — real, but not here

Never negative, paid by both sides, and structurally incapable of paying you to wait
(`jupiter-perps-trading.md` §2). It is the dominant cost above roughly 15 hours for longs
(`LEVERAGE-ECONOMICS.md` §3). Below an hour it is noise, per §0 Fact 2. **A short-horizon
strategy that models carry carefully and turnover casually has optimised the wrong term.**

### The fixed term — small, except when it isn't

Priority fees and Jito tips are fixed dollar amounts per transaction, so their cost in bps
falls as notional rises:

| Notional | base fee only | modest priority | contested/Jito |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $500 | 0.01 bps | 0.40 bps | 20.00 bps |
| $2,000 | 0.00 bps | 0.10 bps | 5.00 bps |
| $10,000 | 0.00 bps | 0.02 bps | 1.00 bps |
| $50,000 | 0.00 bps | 0.00 bps | 0.20 bps |
| $250,000 | 0.00 bps | 0.00 bps | 0.04 bps |

**The non-obvious finding: this is the one term where leverage genuinely helps.** Leverage
raises notional for fixed collateral, so it amortises a fixed dollar cost across a larger
position. At $500 notional a contested-priority round trip costs **20 bps — more than the
venue's own 12 bps fee** — while at $250,000 it is 0.04 bps and disappears.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. First, **a small account trading in contention
faces a materially different cost structure than a large one**, and a backtest calibrated
on 12 bps understates a $500 account's cost by more than half. Second, this is the *only*
respect in which high leverage improves the economics of this family, and it is a second-
order effect against the first-order barrier cost in §6. It is not a reason to use leverage.

### Not in any of the three terms

Each of the following makes the picture worse, never better, and none is modelled:

- **Price-impact fee.** Computable in its linear component only; the additive component's
  `factor`, `exp`, and imbalance threshold are per-custody on-chain values Jupiter does not
  publish. **A trader cannot precompute total price impact from public documentation.**
- **Failed transactions**, which cost the network fee and deliver no position.
- **The liquidation penalty itself** — liquidation forfeits 100% of remaining collateral,
  so §6's "time to reach the barrier" is the optimistic reading.
- **Sandwich/MEV costs**, which are a swap-side concern rather than a perps concern on an
  oracle-priced venue, but apply to any collateral routed through a swap.

## 4. Axis C — Edge sources, filtered by the toll

This is the axis that a taxonomy-first map gets wrong. The question is not "what strategies
exist" but "what could plausibly produce more than ~12 bps of edge per round trip, at this
venue, at this horizon."

The filter is the required-win-rate table. For a symmetric ±*m* trade paying the toll:

> `required_win_rate = 0.5 + toll / (2·move)`

| Target move | Required win rate |
|---|---:|
| 0.05% | — impossible |
| 0.10% | — impossible |
| 0.20% | 80.1% |
| 0.50% | 62.0% |
| 1.00% | 56.0% |
| 2.00% | 53.0% |

(Toll = 12.03 bps: the 12 bps fee, one minute of carry, and a modest priority fee on
$10,000 notional.)

**Read the top two rows carefully.** A strategy targeting a 5 or 10 bps move cannot break
even at *any* win rate, including 100%, because **the toll exceeds the target move itself**
— winning every single trade still nets `move − toll < 0`. This is not a statement about
skill. It is a statement about arithmetic, and it eliminates the entire class of "capture a
tick" strategies at a stroke.

With that filter in hand:

| Edge source | Mechanism | Verdict at this venue |
|---|---|---|
| **Spread capture / market making** | Quote both sides, earn the bid-ask | **Undefined.** No book, no maker rebate. Not a tuning problem — no mechanism. |
| **Latency race** | Be first to a known price change | **Not available to the trader.** The keeper submits the fill; no FIFO guarantee. |
| **Tick scalping** (< 20 bps targets) | Many tiny captures | **Arithmetically dead.** No win rate suffices. |
| **Sub-minute mean reversion** | Overreaction snaps back | Needs ≥ 0.2% targets at 80% accuracy, or ≥ 0.5% at 62%. Possible in principle; **unmeasured here**. |
| **Oracle-path prediction** | Anticipate the next oracle print | The only edge the venue's structure actually permits. Requires modelling oracle cadence and the Edge/Chainlink/Pyth selection logic — **whose staleness window and deviation threshold are unpublished.** |
| **Liquidation-cascade proximity** | Trade the forced-flow impulse | Impulses are large enough to clear the toll. But cascade timing is precisely when the *barrier* is closest, so the edge and the ruin risk are the same event. A strategy card exists (`liquidation_cascade_proximity`) — unmeasured at this frequency. |
| **Perp-spot / cross-venue basis** | Dislocation between venues | Clears the toll only when the dislocation exceeds the toll on *both* legs. `dex_cex_dislocation` and `triangular_arbitrage` cards exist. Execution risk is the binding constraint, not signal quality. |
| **Funding-rate harvesting** | Collect the funding payment | **Structurally impossible here.** Jupiter has a borrow fee, not a funding rate: it is never negative and nobody is ever paid to wait. Porting this from a funding-rate venue is a modelling error, not a strategy. |
| **Event-driven (scheduled)** | Trade a dated release | Turnover is bounded by event frequency, so the toll is paid rarely. **The most toll-compatible member of the family** — and the one least like "extremely short term high margin" as usually imagined. |

The pattern across the column: **the edge sources that survive the toll are the ones with
low natural turnover, and the ones that motivate high leverage are the ones with high
turnover.** The family's name contains its own contradiction.

## 5. Axis D — The Jupiter execution surface

Five venue behaviours that a short-horizon design must accommodate. Each is a documented
fact from the skill references, not an inference.

1. **Two transactions per action, unknown latency.** Request PDA, then keeper fulfilment.
   No TTL. No published latency figure.
2. **No FIFO ordering.** Jupiter states that a trigger transaction and a liquidation
   transaction may hit the chain simultaneously and "whichever is processed first by the
   network will execute. The outcome cannot be guaranteed." **A stop-loss is therefore not
   a guarantee of exit ahead of liquidation** — which is the single most important thing to
   internalise before running anything leveraged at short horizon.
3. **Slippage guard cancels rather than fills worse.** Protective for price, but it makes
   the executed trade set conditional on market quiescence.
4. **New limit orders are blocked when market utilisation exceeds 80%**, and above 150×
   leverage the UI warns that limit orders "may not execute as expected." Both bind exactly
   when volatility is highest — i.e. when a short-horizon strategy most wants them.
5. **Payout denomination.** Longs are collateralised in the underlying and paid
   `closingValueUsd / tokenPriceAtClose`, so a profitable SOL long receives fewer SOL the
   higher SOL goes. Collateral USD value is fixed at deposit.

For the surrounding integration questions — how a signal reaches this venue, why Jupiter
offers no webhooks or push notifications for fills, and why fill detection is therefore a
polling problem — see `docs/trading-signals-concept-family.md`, which maps that axis in
full and is current as of 2026-08-04.

## 6. Axis E — The absorbing barrier as a short-horizon constraint

The barrier is the reason "high margin" and "extremely short term" interact worse than
either does alone.

| Leverage | Adverse move to liquidate | Time for 1σ to reach it | Barrier in σ of a 1-min move |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2x | 50.00% | 186.2 d | 517.8σ |
| 5x | 20.00% | 29.8 d | 207.1σ |
| 10x | 10.00% | 7.4 d | 103.6σ |
| 20x | 5.00% | 1.9 d | 51.8σ |
| 50x | 2.00% | 7.2 h | 20.7σ |
| 100x | 1.00% | 1.8 h | 10.4σ |
| 250x | 0.40% | 17 min | 4.1σ |

> **This table rests on an assumption, not a measurement.** It uses 70% annualised
> volatility with driftless square-root-of-time scaling. That figure is an order-of-
> magnitude stand-in for SOL, **not** something this repo has measured — see §7. Re-run
> with `--vol` to see the sensitivity; the qualitative conclusion holds across any
> plausible value, but no specific cell should be quoted as a fact.

Three readings:

**The barrier crosses into the trading horizon somewhere around 50×.** Below 20× the
barrier is days away and is a risk-management concern. At 100× and above it is inside the
holding period itself and becomes a *design* concern — the strategy is implicitly a bet
that noise stays small for the duration.

**Expected value is the wrong objective in this region.** An absorbing barrier makes the
process path-dependent: a strategy that is right about the distribution of outcomes can
still be removed before the distribution is realised. This is the failure mode
`liquidation-as-an-absorbing-barrier.md` names as "the strategy is removed from the game
while its thesis is still intact."

**The barrier drifts toward you.** Borrow fees accrue against collateral, so the
liquidation price moves in the adverse direction while you hold, even with price flat
(`LEVERAGE-ECONOMICS.md` §2). At short horizons the drift is small in absolute terms — but
so is the distance to the barrier at high leverage, which is exactly when it matters.

**The interaction with §4 that deserves its own sentence:** the edge source with the
largest short-horizon impulse (liquidation cascades) is the one that moves price fastest,
and therefore the one most likely to reach a nearby barrier. At high leverage, the trade
that pays best and the trade that ruins you are the same trade.

## 7. What this repo cannot currently test — and why that matters

**Every empirical result in `research/` is computed on 1-hour bars.** The 9-coin sweep, the
CPCV work, the walk-forward analysis, the cross-asset transfer study: all 1h.

A one-minute strategy is **not evaluable on one-hour bars.** Three specific failures, in
increasing order of severity:

1. **The signal does not exist at that resolution.** A sub-minute reversion signal has no
   representation in a 1h bar.
2. **Turnover cannot be represented.** The fee model's third multiplier — trade count — is
   the dominant cost term (§3), and a 1h bar can produce at most one round trip per hour.
3. **Intrabar path is invisible, and it is exactly what matters.** At 100× the barrier is
   1.0%. A 1h bar that opens and closes within 1.0% may still have travelled well past it
   intrabar. **A backtest on 1h bars will systematically under-report liquidations**, and
   the under-reporting grows worse precisely as leverage rises. A high-leverage backtest on
   hourly data is not a conservative approximation; it is a biased one, biased favourably.

**What would be needed:** 1-minute or tick OHLCV for the traded asset, and a fill model
that respects the two-transaction keeper flow rather than assuming a fill at the signal
bar's price. The repo has **neither**, and acquiring the first without the second would
produce a confidently wrong number rather than an honest one.

This section exists because the repo's own standing rule is that the danger in `research/`
is overstating a result. **Nothing in this document should be read as evidence that any
short-horizon strategy works or fails empirically.** §§3–6 are closed-form arithmetic over
published venue parameters plus one labelled volatility assumption; they establish what a
strategy would have to *overcome*, never what one would *earn*. The distinction is the
whole point.

## 8. Frontier and open questions

Genuinely unsettled things in this space, distinguished from things that are merely
unmeasured here.

- **JupUSD and the stable-custody rate.** A new stable custody changes short-side carry.
  Negligible below an hour, but it changes the §3 crossover. **UNVERIFIED** — current rates
  must be read from live custody accounts.
- **Oracle cadence as a tradeable object.** If the Edge/Chainlink/Pyth selection logic has
  a characteristic update interval, the oracle path is partly predictable — which is the
  one edge source the venue structure permits (§4). The staleness window and deviation
  threshold are **unpublished**, so this is currently unresearchable from public data.
- **Whether keeper latency is stable or fat-tailed.** A stable delay is a modelling
  constant; a fat-tailed one is a risk factor that dominates every other term at short
  horizon. **Unmeasured, and measurable** — this is the highest-value empirical question in
  the document, and it requires only live observation rather than historical data.
- **Priority-fee dynamics as a cost distribution rather than a constant.** §3 treats the
  fixed cost as three static scenarios. In contention it is a distribution correlated with
  exactly the volatility that generates the trading signal — meaning the cost rises when
  the edge appears. That correlation, if real, would tighten §4's filter further.
- **Whether the 80%-utilisation limit-order block is binding in practice.** It gates a
  risk-management tool at the moment it is most needed (§5), but its empirical frequency is
  unknown.

## 10. The summary, if you read nothing else

1. **Turnover kills this family, not leverage.** One-minute turnover costs 173% of
   collateral per day in fees at 1×.
2. **Market making and tick scalping are not slow or unprofitable here — they are
   undefined.** No book means no spread; a sub-20 bps target cannot break even at any win
   rate.
3. **The only edge the venue's structure permits is oracle-path prediction**, and the
   parameters needed to model the oracle are unpublished.
4. **Above ~50× the liquidation barrier is inside the holding period**, so the strategy is
   a bet on noise rather than on signal.
5. **This repo cannot test any of it**, because 1-hour bars hide the intrabar path that
   determines whether a high-leverage position survives — and they hide it in the
   flattering direction.
