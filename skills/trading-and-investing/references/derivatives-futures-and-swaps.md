# Derivatives: Futures, Forwards & Swaps (spoke)

A spoke of the **`trading-and-investing`** hub. It owns every derivative **except options** — futures, forwards, swaps, and the crypto-native perpetual. Options are the sibling spoke, `options-trading-and-strategies`, because optionality (a right rather than an obligation) changes the payoff from linear to convex and requires an entirely separate apparatus (the Greeks).

> **Educational information only — NOT financial, investment, tax, or legal advice, and NOT a recommendation to trade anything.** Futures and swaps are **leveraged obligations**. Because you post a fraction of notional as margin and are **bound on both sides**, losses are not capped at what you deposited: **you can lose more than your account balance and be liable for the deficit**. A futures position can be **liquidated by the broker without your consent** to meet a margin call. Retail participation in swaps is largely restricted to **eligible contract participants**; most retail exposure comes through ETFs or a perpetual on a crypto venue. Rules, margins, product specs, and tax treatment change — every volatile claim is stamped **as of 2026**; verify with the primary source (CFTC, NFA, CME Group, SEC, ISDA, BIS) before acting.

## 1. The taxonomy, and the one axis that generates it

A derivative is a **contract whose value derives from an underlying** asset, rate, or index. The single most useful thing to internalise: **the instrument is the contract, not the underlying.** You do not own oil; you own a promise about oil.

The classical family has four members, generated by two independent binary splits:

| | **Standardized, exchange-traded, CCP-cleared** | **Customized, OTC, bilateral** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Both sides obligated** | **Futures** | **Forwards**, **Swaps** |
| **One side holds a right** | **Listed options** → sibling spoke | OTC/exotic options |

**Axis 1 — obligation vs right** is the deeper cut. A futures buyer *must* transact; an option buyer *may*. That is why futures payoffs are linear in the underlying (delta ≈ 1, no gamma) and options are not.

**Axis 2 — standardized/cleared vs customized/bilateral.** Futures and forwards are economically near-identical trades that differ almost entirely on this axis. Standardization is what makes a central counterparty possible, and the CCP is what removes the need to assess who is on the other side.

A **swap** is best understood not as a fifth thing but as **a portfolio of forwards** — a fixed-for-floating interest-rate swap is a strip of forward rate agreements bundled into one contract with one master agreement.[^bis-otc]

## 2. Contract specification — the spec *is* the product

For a futures contract the **specification** carries information that in equities you would never have to think about. Two contracts on the same commodity with different specs are different products with different prices and different risks. Every spec answers:

- **Contract size / multiplier.** CME E-mini S&P 500 (ES) is $50 × the index; Micro E-mini (MES) is $5 ×. WTI Crude (CL) is 1,000 barrels.[^cme-es][^cme-cl]
- **Tick size and tick value.** The minimum price increment and what it is worth. ES trades in 0.25 index-point ticks = $12.50 per tick.[^cme-es]
- **Delivery months and the expiry calendar.** Quarterly (H/M/U/Z = Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec) for equity index; monthly for energy.
- **Settlement method** — physical delivery or cash. This is the spec line that has caused the most retail damage (§5).
- **Last trading day, first notice day.** For a physically-delivered contract, *first notice day* is when you can be assigned a delivery obligation. Retail traders must be flat before it.
- **Position limits and accountability levels**, set by the exchange and CFTC.
- **Trading hours** — nearly 24×5 for the majors, which matters because a gap can happen while equity markets are shut.

**Why this section comes before margin:** the commonest expensive surprise in futures is not leverage, it is discovering that the multiplier is 1,000× and the position is 40 times larger than intended.

## 3. Margin: a performance bond, not a loan

**This is the single most-confused point for anyone arriving from equities, and it is worth stating precisely.**

In an equity **margin account** you *borrow money* from the broker to buy stock. You pay **interest** on the debit balance. Reg T governs it; the loan is real.

In **futures**, margin is **not a loan and you pay no interest on it.** It is a **performance bond** — a good-faith deposit that you will honour a contract you have entered into. Nothing has been lent because nothing has been bought yet; you have promised a future transaction.[^cme-margin][^cftc-margin]

Consequences that follow from that distinction and surprise people:

- **Initial margin** is set by the exchange clearing house (with brokers free to require more), and is calibrated to cover a plausible one-day move — SPAN/SPAN 2 methodology at CME.[^cme-margin]
- **Maintenance margin** is a lower threshold. Fall below it and you get a **margin call**; fail to meet it and the broker may liquidate **without your consent**.
- Margin is **not a down payment** and does not bound your loss. Notional exposure is many multiples
  of it: an ES contract at index 5,000 is **$250,000 notional against roughly $13,000-$15,000 of
  exchange initial margin (as of 2026 — verify at CME's ES margins page)**, i.e. on the order of
  **20x** leverage. The $400-$2,000 figures brokers advertise are **intraday day-trade margin**, not
  the exchange requirement — see the day-trading bullet below, and do not conflate them.[^cme-es-margin]
- Because margin is a bond and not a purchase, **there is no equivalent of "I'll just hold it until it recovers."** Variation margin (§4) demands cash daily.
- **Day-trading margin** offered by brokers intraday is far lower than overnight/exchange margin, which quietly encourages the largest positions at the least-capitalised moment.

**Leverage is the ratio of notional to margin, and it is not a setting you choose** — it is whatever the exchange's margin requirement implies, unless you deliberately over-collateralise.

## 4. Daily mark-to-market and variation margin

A futures position is **settled in cash every day**, not at expiry. After the close, the clearing house computes each account's gain or loss against the **daily settlement price** and moves cash: **variation margin**.[^cme-margin]

This has three consequences a forward does not share:

1. **Your paper loss becomes a cash obligation the same day.** A position that is "right eventually" can still bankrupt you, because the intermediate path is funded in real money. This is the mechanism behind most famous derivatives failures — Metallgesellschaft's 1993 oil programme was **argued by Culp & Miller (1995) to be economically
sound** and to have failed on margin calls against the futures leg while the offsetting physical
exposure produced no cash — though **Mello & Parsons (1995) argue the stack-and-roll hedge was itself
flawed**, far from minimum-variance and dependent on the crude curve staying in backwardation. Read it
as a funding-liquidity failure **compounded by a curve bet**, not a pure timing accident.[^mg]
2. **Credit risk is truncated daily.** The CCP never lets an exposure accumulate; this is precisely why a clearing house can guarantee both sides.
3. **P&L is marked to market annually for tax, not continuously.** Open §1256 positions are marked at
   **year end** whether or not closed (§12), so unrealised futures gains do not defer the way an
   unsold equity position does. Daily variation margin is a cash movement, **not** a taxable event
   per day.

**Practical reading:** in futures, *funding your losses is part of the strategy*, and a strategy that ignores the cash-flow timing of variation margin is not a complete strategy.

## 5. Settlement: physical vs cash, and the negative-price lesson

- **Cash-settled** — at expiry the contract pays the difference against a reference price. No commodity moves. ES, most index futures, and most crypto futures are cash-settled.
- **Physically delivered** — the short must deliver the actual commodity and the long must take it, at a specified delivery point. WTI Crude (CL) delivers at Cushing, Oklahoma.[^cme-cl]

**Why a retail trader must care.** In **April 2020**, the expiring May WTI contract settled at **−$37.63/barrel**: holders of the physically-delivered contract needed somewhere to put oil, Cushing storage was effectively full, and the right to receive a barrel became a liability.[^cftc-neg-oil] Some retail participants holding oil-tracking products discovered that "oil can't go below zero" was a statement about a commodity, not about a **contract to receive** one.

**The general lesson.** A physically-delivered contract carries an obligation that has nothing to do with your price view. Know the **first notice day**, and know whether your product is the front-month contract or a basket.

## 6. Basis, convergence, and the term structure

**Basis = spot price − futures price** (conventions vary; state yours). At expiry, arbitrage forces basis to zero — **convergence**. That enforced convergence is what makes a future a hedge rather than a separate bet.

Across expiries, the curve has a shape:

- **Contango** — further-dated contracts are *more expensive*. Normal for storables, because holding the physical costs money: **cost of carry** = storage + insurance + financing.
- **Backwardation** — further-dated contracts are *cheaper*. Signals scarcity now, or a **convenience yield** for having the physical in hand.

For a financial future the relationship is tight: **F ≈ S · e^(r−q)T**, where *r* is the financing rate and *q* the dividend/income yield. For commodities it is looser, because storage and convenience yield are not observable.

## 7. Roll yield — why a commodity ETF can bleed in a flat market

A futures position has an expiry; maintaining continuous exposure means **rolling** — closing the front contract and opening a later one.

- **In contango**, you sell the cheap expiring contract and buy a dearer one: you hold *fewer* units of exposure each roll. Repeated, this is a persistent drag — **negative roll yield**.
- **In backwardation**, the roll is accretive.

This is the mechanism by which a commodity ETF can lose money over a year in which the **spot price was unchanged**. It is not a fee and it is not tracking error in the usual sense; it is the term structure being harvested against you.

**Where this bites:** any product that promises "exposure to oil/VIX/natural gas" via front-month futures. Volatility products are the extreme case, because VIX futures are in contango most of the time.

## 8. Forwards and OTC counterparty risk

A **forward** is a futures contract without the exchange: bilateral, customised in size, date and terms, and **not centrally cleared** by default.

What you gain: an exact hedge. A corporate treasurer needing €13.4M on 17 March gets precisely that, where futures would offer standard sizes and quarterly dates (**basis risk** — hedging with an imperfectly matched instrument).

What you take on:

- **Counterparty risk.** No CCP stands between you. If the dealer fails before settlement, you hold a claim, not a hedge.
- **No daily mark-to-market** in the classic form, so exposure *accumulates* rather than being truncated daily. Modern practice mitigates this with **CSA collateral agreements** under an **ISDA Master Agreement**.[^isda-master]
- **Illiquidity.** Exiting means negotiating with the same dealer, who knows you need out.

**Non-deliverable forwards (NDFs)** are the OTC answer for currencies with capital controls — cash-settled in USD against a fixing rate because the local currency cannot be delivered offshore.

## 9. Swaps

A **swap** exchanges two streams of future cash flows. Notional is usually **never exchanged** — it is a scaling reference for the payments.

**Interest-rate swap (IRS)** — the largest category by notional. One side pays fixed, the other floating on a benchmark; only the **net** difference moves on each reset. Uses: a borrower with floating-rate debt swaps to fixed to remove rate risk.
**The benchmark changed.** USD **LIBOR** ceased publication for remaining tenors on **30 June 2023**; USD swaps reference **SOFR**, a secured overnight rate. Any pre-2022 material describing LIBOR swaps as current is stale.[^arrc-libor]

**FX swap** — a spot leg plus an offsetting forward leg in the opposite direction, with **no interim
interest payments**; the rate differential is embedded in the forward points. Predominantly
short-dated funding and rollover, and the largest FX instrument by turnover.

**Cross-currency swap** — exchanges principal at inception and maturity **and** periodic interest in
both currencies over the life. Distinguish both from an **FX forward**, a single future exchange. BIS
reports the first two as separate instruments and conflating them is common.[^bis-otc]

**Commodity swap** — fixed-for-floating on a commodity index; how an airline hedges jet fuel without taking delivery.

**Total return swap (TRS)** — one side receives the total return (price + income) of an asset, paying a financing leg. The receiver gets economic exposure **without appearing as the holder of record**. That opacity is the point and the danger: **Archegos Capital Management** built enormous concentrated single-name exposure through TRS with multiple prime brokers, none of whom could see the whole position; its 2021 collapse cost the banks well over $10bn, with Credit Suisse alone taking ~$5.5bn.[^cs-archegos] The instrument did not fail — the *disclosure* did.

**Credit default swap (CDS)** — protection against a credit event. The buyer pays a periodic premium (the **spread**) and receives compensation if the reference entity defaults. Economically insurance, structurally a derivative, and critically **you need not own the underlying bond**, which is why CDS notional can exceed the debt
outstanding. A **separate** danger is one-way *written* exposure: AIG Financial Products had sold
protection on super-senior CDO tranches, and escalating **CSA collateral calls** as marks fell —
compounded by a ratings-downgrade trigger — is what required the 2008 federal rescue. The
no-ownership feature and the AIG failure are two different problems, routinely run together.

**Post-crisis regulation (US).** Dodd-Frank Title VII imposed, for standardised swaps: a **central clearing** mandate, **exchange/SEF trading**, and **reporting to swap data repositories**. Jurisdiction is split — CFTC over most swaps, SEC over security-based swaps.[^dodd-frank] Uncleared swaps face **UMR** initial-margin requirements. Retail access is limited: most swaps require **eligible contract participant** status.

## 10. The perpetual — a fifth primitive

The classical taxonomy has no slot for the **perpetual future** ("perp"), the dominant crypto derivative. It is a futures-like, cash-settled, both-sides-obligated contract with **no expiry date**.

**Removing expiry breaks convergence.** A dated future is tethered to spot because arbitrage forces basis to zero *at settlement*. With no settlement, nothing enforces the tether — so a perpetual must **engineer a replacement**:

- **Funding rate** (the usual design). Periodically, longs pay shorts or shorts pay longs, in proportion to how far the perp trades from an index price. If the perp is rich, longs pay — which subsidises the arbitrage that pushes it back. Funding is **bidirectional** and can be negative. **Popularised by BitMEX's XBTUSD (May 2016)**, the first widely traded crypto perpetual swap — though the instrument was **proposed by Robert Shiller in 1992/93**, which BitMEX's own materials credited. "Introduced by" would overstate it. Now near-universal on centralised venues.[^shiller]
- **Borrow-fee designs.** Some venues, notably **Jupiter Perps** on Solana, charge a **utilization-driven borrow fee instead**. It is **not** a funding rate and confusing the two is the family's most-cited trader-side error: **both sides always pay, the rate is never negative, and nobody is ever paid to wait.** Depth → `jupiter-perps-trading` and `jupiter-perps-leverage-and-liquidation`.

**Structural consequences worth carrying:**
- A perp has **no roll** — so no roll yield, and none of §7's drag. The cost shows up as a continuous funding/borrow stream instead.
- **The carry cost is path-dependent and unbounded in time.** A dated future's carry is capped by its remaining life; a perp's is not.
- **Liquidation, not delivery, is the terminal event.** With no expiry, the only forced exit is a margin failure — so liquidation-engine design *is* the product. On a borrow-fee venue the **liquidation price drifts** as fees accrue against collateral.
- Venue risk is first-order **on offshore and on-chain venues**: no CCP, no regulator standing behind
  the guarantee. **As of 2026 that is no longer the whole picture.** CFTC-regulated, DCO-cleared
  perpetual futures are now listed in the US (Bitnomial self-certified the first, April 2025; Coinbase
  Financial Markets launched for US customers 21 July 2025; the CFTC approved Kalshi's BTCPERP on
  29 May 2026), and **CME sued the CFTC on 18 June 2026 arguing a perpetual is a *swap* under the CEA
  rather than a future.** Whether a perp is legally a future or a swap is **unsettled** — which
  sharpens rather than weakens this section's point that the classical taxonomy has no clean slot for
  it. Verify current status before relying on either classification.[^perp-us]

Perp mechanics on centralised venues → `crypto-and-digital-asset-trading`; on-chain perp DEXs → `defi-and-onchain-trading`.

## 11. Hedging vs speculation, and what a hedge actually costs

The same contract serves both. What differs is whether you hold an **offsetting exposure**.

A **hedger** has the risk already — a farmer with a crop, a treasurer with foreign receivables. Selling futures converts an uncertain future price into a known one. **A hedge does not make money; it removes variance, and it removes the upside too.** If the crop price doubles, the farmer forgoes it. That is the price of certainty, not a failure of the hedge.

A **speculator** has no offsetting exposure and takes the risk for the expected return, providing the liquidity the hedger needs.

Three honest complications:

- **Basis risk.** Unless the contract matches your exposure exactly, the hedge leaves a residual. Hedging jet fuel with heating-oil futures leaves the *spread* unhedged.
- **Cash-flow risk.** Variation margin on the hedge is due daily; the offsetting physical gain may not be realised for months (§4). A hedge can be economically right and still fail on funding.
- **Hedge accounting and reporting** can make an effective hedge look like a loss in a given period, which creates pressure to lift it at the worst time.

## 12. Tax and regulatory seams

**US tax (as of 2026 — verify; this is a pointer, detail → `trading-regulation-compliance-and-taxes`, which is **declared in the hub routing table but not yet built** as of 2026 — go to IRS Topic 429 and Pub. 550 directly).** **Section 1256** covers regulated futures contracts and broad-based index options: positions are **marked to market at year end** whether or not closed, and gains are taxed **60% long-term / 40% short-term regardless of holding period**.[^irs-1256] For a frequent trader this is often favourable versus all-short-term equity treatment. **Crypto perpetuals on offshore or on-chain venues are generally *not* §1256 contracts**; do not assume 60/40 — crypto P&L → `onchain-pnl-and-tax-accounting`.

**Regulator.** Futures and most swaps are **CFTC**, with **NFA** as the SRO — *not* the SEC, and **SIPC does not generally cover futures positions** — SIPC coverage is defined by SIPA, and futures
in a commodity account fall outside it (a narrow exception exists for futures carried in an
SEC-approved portfolio-margining *securities* account).[^sipc] Security-based swaps are SEC. Check a
futures broker (FCM) on **NFA BASIC** rather than BrokerCheck.[^cftc-margin]

## 13. Anti-patterns

1. **Treating futures margin as a down payment.** It bounds neither your loss nor your obligation.
2. **Holding a physically-delivered contract past first notice day.** See §5.
3. **Assuming a commodity ETF tracks spot.** Roll yield (§7) is a structural drag in contango, not tracking error.
4. **Sizing on margin instead of notional.** The position is the notional. Always compute it explicitly.
5. **Treating a perpetual's funding as a fee.** It is a bidirectional cash flow that can pay you — and on a borrow-fee venue it is *not* bidirectional, which is the opposite error.
6. **Assuming a perp's cost is bounded.** No expiry means no cap on accumulated carry.
7. **Ignoring the cash-flow path of a hedge.** Being right at expiry does not survive a margin call in month three.
8. **Reading pre-2023 LIBOR swap material as current.** USD LIBOR ended 30 June 2023.
9. **Assuming OTC means unregulated.** Post-Dodd-Frank, standardised swaps are cleared, SEF-traded and reported.
10. **Confusing notional with exposure in TRS/CDS.** Archegos and AIG are both stories about notional nobody could see.
