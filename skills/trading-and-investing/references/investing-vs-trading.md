# Investing vs Trading (foundation — the central distinction)

The single organizing idea of this family: what separates **investing** from **trading**, the trading-styles spectrum, the active/passive axis, the high-level tax angle, and the **load-bearing evidence** that most active retail traders underperform. This is what tells a reader which side of the family they actually want.

> Educational information, NOT financial or tax advice. The evidence below is about *populations* of traders, not a prediction about any one person. Figures dated **as of 2026**; verify with the regulator/IRS.

## The defining differences

**Investing** puts money to work over a long horizon to build wealth gradually — through compounding, dividends/interest, and the long-term upward drift of markets ("time in the market"). The SEC frames it plainly: "for most people, the only way to attain financial security is to save and invest over a long period of time."[^1] Goal: wealth accumulation toward life goals; horizon: years to decades; decisions driven by fundamentals and asset allocation.[^1][^2]

**Trading** buys and sells frequently to profit from shorter-term price moves ("timing the market"). Positions are held "minutes to months, aiming for more frequent but smaller profits," typically driven by technical analysis and price action rather than fundamentals.[^2][^3]

| Dimension | Investing | Trading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Time horizon** | Long-term: years to decades[^2][^3] | Short-term: minutes to months[^2][^3] |
| **Core goal** | Build wealth (compounding, dividends)[^2][^3] | Profit from short-term price moves[^2][^3] |
| **What drives decisions** | Fundamentals, asset allocation, "time in the market"[^2][^3] | Price action, technical analysis, "timing the market"[^2][^3] |
| **Activity level** | Low — periodic check-ins[^3] | High — frequent trading; can be full-time[^3] |
| **Typical risk profile** | Generally lower; can weather downturns over long horizons[^2][^3] | Generally higher, often leveraged; strict risk management required[^2][^3] |

A common framing: trading can be seen as a *type* of activity within the broader universe of investing, with **timing** the single biggest differentiator.[^3]

## The spectrum of trading styles

Trading styles form a continuum defined chiefly by holding period (longest → shortest). Overview only — `trading-strategies-and-styles` owns the detail:[^4]

- **Position trading** — longest-horizon trading; positions held **weeks to months** (sometimes longer).
- **Swing trading** — medium-term; **several days to weeks**, capturing intermediate "swings."
- **Day trading** — **intraday**; positions typically closed by the market close (**no overnight holding**).
- **Scalping** — shortest; **seconds to minutes**, many trades a day for small per-trade gains.

(Ordering is consistent across sources; the day/week/month boundaries are approximate.)

## Active vs passive
<a id="active-vs-passive"></a>

A **separate axis** from investing-vs-trading, though correlated:

- **Active management** tries to beat a benchmark via security selection and/or timing (the style most overlapping with "trading").[^5]
- **Passive (index) investing** simply tracks a broad index at low cost, accepting the market's average return — the approach the Bogleheads philosophy champions ("buy widely diversified funds… stay the course").[^6]

A long-term *investor* can still be active (a stock-picker holding for years) or passive (index funds); investing-vs-trading is about **time horizon and intent**, while active-vs-passive is about **whether you try to beat the market or match it**.[^5][^6]

The active-side evidence is sobering: per S&P Dow Jones Indices' **SPIVA** scorecards, the majority of active managers underperform their benchmarks over long horizons — over the 20 years through 2024, **~94% of all domestic US funds underperformed** the S&P Composite 1500.[^5] The failure rate generally rises with the time horizon. *(SPIVA is the standard reference, corroborated by Morningstar's active/passive barometer.)*[^5]

## The tax angle
<a id="the-tax-angle"></a>

In the US, capital-gains tax depends on holding period (as of 2026):[^7]

- **Short-term capital gains** — assets held **one year or less** — taxed as **ordinary income** (federal brackets 10%–37%).
- **Long-term capital gains** — held **more than one year** — preferential rates of **0%, 15%, or 20%** by income (plus a possible 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax).

**Implication:** frequent trading realizes gains inside the one-year window, so it tends to be **less tax-efficient** than long-term buy-and-hold, compounding with higher transaction costs.[^7] *(A deeper spoke — `trading-regulation-compliance-and-taxes` — owns wash sales, lot selection, trader-tax-status, NIIT thresholds, state tax. Educational only, not tax advice; figures change yearly — verify at irs.gov. The exact bracket breakpoints shift annually; the short-vs-long-term *structure* is stable.)*

## Evidence underperform
<a id="evidence-underperform"></a>

**One of the most consistently documented findings in household finance** — corroborated across multiple independent academic datasets and regulators:

- **Barber & Odean (2000), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth," *Journal of Finance*.** Among 66,465 US households at a discount broker (1991–96), those who **traded the most earned 11.4% annually vs the market's 17.9%**; stocks bought subsequently underperformed stocks sold, not even covering trading costs. Attributed to **overconfidence**.[^8]
- **Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean (Taiwan, 1992–2006).** Across the entire market, day traders' **aggregate performance is negative, the vast majority unprofitable**; **well under 1% are predictably profitable** after costs; ~74% of day-trading volume comes from traders with a history of losses.[^9] A related paper in the *Review of Asset Pricing Studies* states **97% of day traders are likely to lose money** going forward; an earlier paper found **more than 8 of 10** lose in a typical six-month period.[^9]
- **Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti (2020), "Day Trading for a Living?" (Brazil).** Of those who began day-trading equity-index futures and persisted **300+ days, 97% lost money**; only ~1.1% earned more than the minimum wage; **no evidence of learning** (performance did not improve with persistence).[^10]
- **Regulator warnings.** FINRA's **Day-Trading Risk Disclosure Statement (Rule 2270)** requires firms to disclose that day trading "can be extremely risky," is "not appropriate for someone of limited resources… and low risk tolerance," and that "you should be prepared to lose all of the funds that you use for day trading."[^11] FINRA's Regulatory Notice 24-13 cites a US Senate report finding "a majority of day traders lose."[^11]

> **Keep the two "97%" figures distinct:** Brazil (Chague et al.) = 97% of those who *persisted >300 days* lost money in index futures; Taiwan ("Learning Fast or Slow?") = 97% *likely to lose going forward*. They are independent findings from different markets and methods — do not merge them into one universal "97% of all traders lose" statistic.

**Confidence:** VERY HIGH that the large majority of active retail/day traders underperform a simple index or lose money (≥4 independent datasets across the US, Taiwan, and Brazil, plus regulators). MEDIUM on any single precise percentage in isolation (figures vary by market, period, cost treatment, and "persisted" filters). The widely-quoted "~72% of day traders end the year with losses" is **FINRA-attributed across financial media but hard to pin to one canonical FINRA publication** — treat as "widely cited," with Rule 2270 and Notice 24-13 as the clean primary sources.[^11]

**Honest counter-evidence (negation finding):** a small skilled minority *do* show persistent skill — the same Barber/Odean team documents high-performing day traders whose returns survive costs, but this group is the **same well-under-1% of all day traders** the underperformance studies identify (i.e., the rare predictably-profitable minority, not a separate, larger group).[^9][^12] The takeaway is not "nobody can win" but "winning is rare, hard, and concentrated in a tiny minority." Likewise, passive buy-and-hold is not superior in *every* window (e.g., the 2000–2009 "lost decade"; sequence-of-returns risk near withdrawal), though historically 20-year US stock returns have been positive.[^12]

## Not mutually exclusive
<a id="not-mutually-exclusive"></a>

The two are not either/or — a person can do both with **different buckets of money**. The common structural expression is the **core-satellite** approach: a **core** of low-cost diversified index funds for long-term compounding, plus a smaller **satellite** sleeve that is actively traded or concentrated.[^13] A trading sleeve "typically only represents a percentage of someone's total investments — not their entire portfolio," letting a person take riskier bets without jeopardizing the long-term core.[^3][^13] (The specific satellite % is illustrative, not prescriptive.)
